# Theory: what can and cannot drive silent errors to zero

2026-11-05. Numbers come from `results/theory_frontier.md` (generated by `vkg/theory_frontier.py`, CPU only). The selective-prediction frontier (T1) is textbook material: Chow's reject option and the risk-coverage analysis of El-Yaniv and Wiener. Neither is re-verified in docs/LITERATURE_CHECK.md, and neither is claimed as new. What is new here is applying it to this project's measured systems, and the conclusions drawn for Goal A.

## T1. The error-yield frontier of any "answer or abstain" system

Take a base system that would answer a question correctly with probability π. A gate keeps an answer when a scalar signal s exceeds a threshold t. Then, over all questions,

- SER(t) = (1 − π) · FPR(t), the kept wrong answers;
- yield(t) = π · TPR(t), the kept correct answers.

FPR and TPR are the false- and true-positive rates of s for "this answer is correct". So the best yield at a target error rate ε is

  **Y\*(ε) = π · ROC(ε / (1 − π)).**

This is an identity, not an approximation. Every gate, prompt-level abstention, trained abstention or verifier that only decides whether to keep an answer is a point under this curve, for the ROC of whatever signal it effectively uses.

With a binormal ROC (separation d′, AUROC A = Φ(d′/√2)), TPR = Φ(d′ + Φ⁻¹(FPR)). As ε → 0, Φ⁻¹(ε/(1−π)) behaves like −√(2 ln(1/ε)). So **for any fixed signal quality, yield goes to 0 as the target error goes to 0.** Zero silent errors with nonzero yield needs d′ → ∞: the wrong and right answers must not overlap at all on the signal.

**Check against our data (T3).** For 4B plain answers on the 300 SimpleQA-E questions, with the S7 probe as the score (AUROC 0.794 for correctness among the answered), the binormal curve predicts the measured best yield within 5 points at every target:

| target SER | measured best yield | binormal prediction |
|---|---|---|
| 1% | 22.7% | 22.0% |
| 3% | 42.3% | 37.4% |
| 7% | 51.3% | 51.3% |
| 10% | 55.7% | 56.3% |

The measured curve picks the best threshold on the same data, so it is slightly optimistic. Our separately designed mechanisms sit on or near this one curve:
- the cautious prompt: 7.0% SER at 53.0% correct, against 51.3% on the curve;
- gate + cautious: 4.0% at 44.7%;
- the S8 DEV check (not a clean test): 10.0% at 58.3%.

This explains the project's recurring observation that "every mechanism buys lower error by answering less": they are all statistical signals of similar quality on the same curve.

## T2. How good a signal must be (binormal)

| base accuracy π | target SER | keep this share of correct answers | AUROC required |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.044 (closed-book 27B) | 1% | 50% | 0.949 |
| 0.593 (4B + retrieval) | 1% | 50% | 0.918 |
| 0.593 | 0.1% | 50% | 0.977 |
| 0.593 | 0.1% | 90% | 0.998 |
| 0.9 | 0.1% | 90% | 0.995 |
| 0.98 | 0.1% | 90% | 0.981 |

Every confidence signal measured in this project falls between 0.64 and 0.94 AUROC, and none comes close to the 0.98–0.998 that a 0.1% target needs. Expected yields at 0.1% SER (T1 table): closed-book 27B 0.1%, 4B + retrieval with an 0.89 signal 8.3%.

**Consequence 1.** Introspective or statistical signals (log-probability, sampling agreement, hidden-state probes, trained abstention) cannot reach Goal A at useful yield. They can only buy points along T1's curve. Two levers are left: raise π, which is what retrieval did (the largest single gain in the project), and replace statistical signals with checks whose error is near zero by construction.

## T4. Certificates: errors that become impossible, and the ones that remain

**Setup.** The system must output each answer y with a certificate κ = (σ, P):
- σ is an exact span of the source text D, with its offsets;
- P is a program from a fixed, deterministic language (identity, date and number parsing, arithmetic) with y = P(σ).

A checker V accepts only if σ occurs in D verbatim and P(σ) evaluates to y. Otherwise the system abstains. Both checks are plain code, as in the formal route and in S8's test A.

Define, for an accepted answer:
- **R** (correct reading): (σ, P) is a correct reading of the question, meaning σ states the asked fact about the asked entity and P derives the asked quantity from it;
- **S** (true source): the statement in σ is true.

**Proposition.**
(a) No accepted answer is unsupported by D: P(accept ∧ y not derivable from D by the language) = 0.
(b) P(silent error) ≤ P(accept ∧ ¬R) + P(accept ∧ R ∧ ¬S).

*Proof.* (a) holds by definition of V. (b): if R and S both hold, σ truly states the asked fact and P derives the asked quantity, so y equals the true answer. A wrong accepted y therefore implies ¬R or ¬S. ∎

The proposition is simple, but it changes what has to be measured:

1. **Fabrication goes to zero by construction.** This is an L3-type property under `target.txt`: it holds for every input, not on average. On our DEV set, the class it removes includes the plain system's answers with no support in the passage. S8 measures the size of that class on fresh data.
2. **Attributed claims depend only on reading.** If the output is phrased "source X says y" rather than "y", its truth needs R only, not S. The silent false-claim rate of attributed output is then exactly P(accept ∧ ¬R), the reading-error rate. Source errors (¬S) stay real, but they become visible: the output names its source, so a false source is no longer a silent claim by the system.
3. **The remaining error is a narrow, trainable task.** R is "this span answers this question". Its base rate among accepted answers is high: on S7b's manual reading, 5 real errors among 146 gated answers, so π ≈ 0.97 at the reading level. T2's last rows show that at π ≈ 0.98 a signal with AUROC ≈ 0.98 reaches 0.1% while keeping 90% of answers. That is still hard, but it is one narrow semantic check rather than open-world truth. Two routes can push it there:
   - checks in code for parts of R: the question's entities occur near σ; the answer type matches the question type (date, number, person); the completeness tests of S8;
   - training aimed at reading (bold direction 2): code-generated passages with near-miss distractors, where the gold answer is known by construction.

## T5. The limit on verifying a low error rate

Even a perfect system has to be shown perfect. With n labelled accepted answers and 0 errors observed, the exact 95% upper bound is about 3/n:

| target error rate among answers | 0 errors seen | 1 error | 3 errors |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5% | 59 | 93 | 153 |
| 1% | 299 | 473 | 773 |
| 0.1% | 2,995 | 4,742 | 7,752 |

**Consequence 2.** Our 200–300-question test sets can at best demonstrate about 1–1.5%. A claim of 0.1% needs about 3,000 accepted answers with gold labels and a grader with near-zero false negatives. Grader false negatives (39.2% of grader-wrong rows in the stratified validation sample) make "wrong" counts upper bounds, and that does not stop a guarantee. Grader false positives, on the other hand, would break one. Any L2 claim (risk control with a finite-sample guarantee, e.g. conformal risk control or a Clopper–Pearson threshold) also assumes the deployment questions are exchangeable with the calibration questions, and it does not survive distribution shift.

## What this implies for the programme

| route | what theory says it can reach | status |
|---|---|---|
| more or better confidence signals, gates, abstention training | a point on T1's curve; 0.1% only with AUROC ≥ 0.98, never seen | studied (S3, S5, S6, S7b); stop investing here |
| raise π: retrieval, better evidence | moves the whole curve up | S2 done; more retrieval work still pays |
| certificates (exact span + code) | fabrication = 0 by construction (L3); attributed-claim error = reading error | S8 measures the filter version; the generation-time version is bold direction 1 |
| reading-specific checks and training | lowers P(¬R) | bold direction 2, plus question-entity and type checks |
| finite-sample guarantee on the residual | L2 at about 1% with 300 calibration answers, 0.1% with about 3,000 | needs a larger labelled set; ROADMAP S4 |

## Limits of this analysis

- **Binormal shape:** T2's required AUROCs assume a binormal ROC, which fits one measured case (T3) but is not guaranteed elsewhere. T1 itself is exact.
- **Optimistic measured curve:** T3's measured curve uses in-sample best thresholds.
- **Base model fixed:** T1 assumes gating only. A system that also changes its answers (retrieval, re-asking) changes π, which is the point of the second lever.
- **R and S are not machine-checkable in general:** the proposition moves the error into them; it does not remove it. What it gains is that one of the two is a narrow, trainable reading task, and the other is made visible by attribution.
